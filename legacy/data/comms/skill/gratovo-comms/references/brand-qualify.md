# Brands — Qualifying

### BRD-QUAL-01 — Reach the person who owns the budget

Rule (must): At small brands (under ~50 people) start at the CEO and work down. At larger brands go to purchasing authority in this order: influencer/digital marketing manager or CMO, then influencer or affiliate specialists, then anyone in marketing, and generic inboxes only as a last resort.
Don't: Pitch assistants or coordinators without budget authority
Source: 2.How to Qualify Influencers - Coach Ammar @16:15 — "For companies under 50 employees, start at the CEO and work down."
Source: 2.How to Qualify Influencers - Coach Ammar — "Avoid assistants, coordinators, and anyone whose role has no budget authority."

### BRD-QUAL-02 — Target brands already paying creators

Rule (must): Only prospect brands that already spend on influencer marketing, so nobody has to be convinced the channel works. Find them in the sponsorships of the top 10-20 creators of the niche (the last 6-12 months or 50-100 videos); brands that sponsor nearly everyone are the best leads.
Do: Search creators' channels for 'code', 'discount', 'checkout' to surface sponsored videos; Record which creator each brand sponsored — it personalizes the pitch
Source: 3.1. Where to find high paying brands — "Target brands that are already investing in influencer marketing."
Source: 3.1. Where to find high paying brands — "Try to double down on getting in touch with these kind of companies that sponsor everyone, this may be your golden nugget."

### BRD-QUAL-03 — Brand audit: 15 minutes, one angle

Rule (must): Before writing, answer two questions in at most 15 minutes: are they doing influencer marketing (no = drop them for now), and who do they work with — the type of creator they ignore is your angle (e.g. they sponsor gym creators but no runners). One sharp observation is enough; research deeper only after they reply.
Source: 3.2. How to Audit Brands - Coach Abu Numaan — "15 minutes, one angle, move on. Go deeper only after they reply."
Source: 3.2. How to Audit Brands - Coach Abu Numaan — "Q1: Are they doing influencer marketing? Yes = keep. No = cut."

### BRD-QUAL-04 — Creator audit before a pitch: three questions, 10 minutes

Rule (must): Before pitching a creator to a brand: where the audience is (tier-1: US, Canada, Australia, UK), how old it is (older usually spends more, but it depends on the product), and their sponsorship history in the last 20 videos (repeat sponsors are a strong signal, one-off sponsors may mean weak results).
Source: 3.3. Auditing Your Influencers - Coach Abu Numaan — "You are looking for Tier 1 countries, primarily the US, Canada, Australia, and the UK."
Source: 3.3. Auditing Your Influencers - Coach Abu Numaan — "Go through the creator's last 20 videos and look for previous sponsorships. This tells you three things: 1. They know how to integrate sponsors."

### BRD-QUAL-05 — Qualification prevents most problems

Rule (must): Most brand problems are qualification problems: a brand without budget, the wrong person, or a misaligned creator. Filter for active spenders, reach the decision maker, and read how a brand responds to tell buyers from window shoppers.
Source: 3.4. Qualifying Your Inf. Brands - Coach Ammar — "Correct qualification pre-handles 90% of issues."
Source: 3.4. Qualifying Your Inf. Brands - Coach Ammar — "Study how brands respond. Look at their engagement patterns."

### BRD-QUAL-07 — Learn their spending range without asking for 'the budget'

Rule (must): Asking for the budget sounds like a setup to take all of it. Ask instead what range they are comfortable with, or whether to include higher-priced creators or cap the options — framed as saving them time with irrelevant options. Price tiers you mention must be real (your roster or market rates).
Source: 3.5. Pre-HandlingHandling objection Aikido Mastery — "Brands can get defensive when you ask about their budget directly."
Source: 3.5. Pre-HandlingHandling objection Aikido Mastery — "Frame budget discussions as a way to help them narrow options, not as a way to extract money."

### BRD-QUAL-08 — Get the budget with logistics questions

Rule (must): Learn the range before sending names or prices, as a logistics question: above or below a price point? what do you usually pay for creators of this size? ROI-focused or top-tier creators? smaller test or established names?
Source: 5.How to Get a Brand's Budget — "To get a brand's budget, your goal is to ask it without them noticing."

Which type suits your campaign better?"

### BRD-QUAL-09 — Building a 300+ brand list

Rule (tip): Take the top 10 creators of the niche (5M+ followers), go through their last 50 videos for sponsors, then repeat across the 30 creators on your own list — that yields hundreds of brands already paying creators.
Source: 5.How to find qualified brands — "To find 300+ qualified brands, check the top 10 influencers in your niche, go through their last 50 pieces of content, and identify sponsors."

### BRD-QUAL-10 — Skip website forms; LinkedIn plus email

Rule (must): Contact forms are a dead end. Find decision makers on LinkedIn (marketing director, influencer marketing manager), message them, and also email them (common formats like first@company.com, or addresses in the privacy policy/terms); a general inbox is fine with a request to be redirected.
Source: 5.Should I apply for forms on websites — "They’re a dead end 99% of the time. Most brands don’t even bother checking them"
Source: 5.Should I apply for forms on websites — "Sending an email alongside your LinkedIn message will increase your chances of getting a response."

### BRD-QUAL-11 — A creator is a platform with a specific audience — pitch brands that need exactly it

Rule (must): Treat each creator as a billboard with a particular crowd in front of it (age, interest, why they watch). Very large general creators suit everyday products (VPNs, food, mobile data); smaller niche creators are worth far more to brands whose product their exact audience needs. Pairing creators and brands at random is what brings back 'let's do affiliate', 'we'll circle back' and silence.
Source: lesson video "3.2. How to Make Attractive Offers" @9:01 — "your influencer is a platform, okay? But he's a platform with specificities. And that's what brands pay for. They pay top dollar for specificities."
Source: lesson video "3.2. How to Make Attractive Offers" @7:31 — "The reason why they're giving you all these objections is because what you're doing is just throwing stuff and hoping something sticks."

### BRD-QUAL-12 — From the audience to the products to the brands

Rule (should): Start from the creator: who watches and why. List the products that audience has to buy (AI may suggest product categories, never brand names). For each category find well-funded brands (bestsellers, premium prices, many reviews) that already pay creators, then their decision makers, and reach out to about ten brands per category before moving to the next category.
Steps: Watch the creator: the audience and why they watch → List the products that audience must buy → Per product: about ten well-funded brands already paying creators → Find the decision makers and reach out → Repeat with the next product category
Do: The personal line can be about one of the brand's products while the pitch is for another product or the whole product area of the same brand; keep it to one idea, never a list of products
Source: lesson video "3.2. How to Make Attractive Offers" @20:09 — "I want to find the best brands to advertise on his platform. Please give me not names of brands because ChatGPT doesn't know. Please give me examples of products that would be perfectly on brand and perfect for this advertisement."
Source: lesson video "3.2. How to Make Attractive Offers" @22:29 — "You want to find the ones that cost a lot of money because that way that means they also have a lot of money."

### BRD-QUAL-13 — Match on the audience behind the screen, not the niche label

Rule (should): Brands buy the audience: first its age, then its country (buying power, shipping, habits; check which countries the brand sells to before leading with an audience share), then its interests and routines. Our niche is defined by our creators, not by product categories: any brand that spends on creators in the niche is a lead, even outside the niche label, when the audience reason holds (a tech creator who is visibly fit and followed partly for it suits a fitness brand). Use it to sell a closed creator to more brands and to bring a brand more creators — always with the audience reason stated.
Don't: Pitch a creator outside the brand's niche on age alone: the audience reason must be specific
Source: lesson video "8.Bonus Upselling influencers with different niche" @4:54 — "when brands pay you, they pay for the eyeballs that are behind the screens. And that's who they're looking for. And the main thing that they look for is the age of the audience, that's the main one."
Source: lesson video "8.Bonus Upselling influencers with different niche" @6:33 — "Once you understand this specifically, it makes everything else much easier. You can go to different brands and sell an influencer that's in a completely different niche."

### BRD-QUAL-14 — Check the brand can and does spend

Rule (should): Before outreach, confirm the brand pays creators (sponsored integrations and posts, not only affiliate links; BRD-QUAL-19) and does so now: a name seen again and again across creators in recent months is active, while a brand last seen sponsoring years ago, or one that stopped months ago, has usually stopped for a reason. Size is a rough guide, not a filter: roughly $50-500M in annual revenue is big enough to spend and small enough to need marketing, but small brands that sponsor creators do pay, so don't over-research revenue, funding or headcount. A role dedicated to influencer or digital marketing is a strong sign of a real budget; affiliate-only brands waste the pipeline.
Source: lesson video "3.4. Qualifying Your Inf. Brands - Coach Ammar" @10:17 — "Usually, what is the golden ratio is from fifty millions to five hundred millions. In revenue, if you have these numbers, that means brands are big enough, they can spend money on influencers small enough that they need marketing, right?"
Source: lesson video "3.4. Qualifying Your Inf. Brands - Coach Ammar" @23:28 — "if you find guys who are dedicated for influencer marketing, that's 100% a good sign they are investing well."

### BRD-QUAL-15 — Beyond brands that already pay creators: brands that pay for attention

Rule (should): Brands that already sponsor creators are the easiest to close, but any brand that would profit from the creator's audience can be pitched. That matters most when one kind of brand has locked up a niche's creators with exclusivity. Look for brands that already buy attention elsewhere: paid ads, sponsored newsletters, blog posts and forum threads in the niche, or high-margin products that can afford the creator's views. The pitch: you are buying attention that way, and this creator gets it for you this way. Build the list from live signals, not from AI-generated brand lists, which are stale.
Source: lesson video "7.2026.00.00.Abu Lahya 2026 Q&A PT 2" @54:20 — "you're not limited to the brands who only work with influencers, they're just easier to close."
Source: lesson video "7.2026.00.00.Abu Lahya 2026 Q&A PT 2" @55:32 — "that's a signal that they're spending money on getting attention, on getting people to look at their stuff. I would reach out to those people and say, hey, you're looking to get attention this way. I can help you get attention that way."

### BRD-QUAL-16 — Only 'no budget' coming back? Target bigger spenders

Rule (should): When brands like the pitch and the creators but keep answering that there is no budget, the outreach is working and the targeting is not. Aim at brands that can spend: more employees, more frequent campaigns, higher-margin products. Keep everything else the same.
Source: lesson video "7.2026.00.00.Abu Lahya 2026 Q&A PT 2" @1:02:12 — "it means your outreach is good, you're just targeting the wrong people. If you are like, I can't afford it, it's just that you're doing everything right, they're just the wrong people. So, I would change what kind of brands are you reaching out to in the first place? Like, how big they are, how many employees they have, how often they do campaigns."

### BRD-QUAL-17 — Who to write to at a brand

Rule (should): Pick the person by title: first anyone with 'influencer' in their title or job description, then partnerships, then social or growth marketing, and the head of marketing (CMO) last. By size: under about 50 employees write to the founder or CEO; between 50 and 200, a marketing manager or head of marketing; above 200, never the CEO. Big brands split work by region, so write to the manager for the creator's audience country. With no named contact, write to support asking who handles partnerships (each email opens a ticket someone must answer), search the website's contact page and privacy policy for addresses, or work out the company's address pattern from one known address.

It influences within the job description. And this may not, that's like the number one person that you want to reach out to. The next one I'll do is like partnerships, um, sometimes like head of partnerships, stuff like this."

### BRD-QUAL-18 — Software and AI brands carry the budgets, and the list goes beyond AI tools

Rule (should): Software, AI and other digital-subscription companies win customers mainly through online marketing and run on high margins, so they usually have real creator budgets, and a software deal ships nothing that can get lost or arrive broken. For AI creators the brand list reaches beyond AI tools: coding tools, productivity apps and other software the same viewers use. Physical-product brands that sponsor the niche are still worth pitching.
Source: coaching call "September 19: Q&A (Advanced Brands) - Osama" (2025-09-19) — "But you can have other software tools to sponsor them. It doesn't have to be just AI tools, right? You can have other coding tools. You can have Notion sponsored them. You can have other productivity stuff as well sponsoring them."
Source: coaching call "All Q/A Calls in November" (2024-11-21) — "because the only way they promote is mainly the only way they can get known is through marketing. not like a physical shop. It's not anything like that. It's all digital. So I think AI companies has a lot of money."

### BRD-QUAL-19 — Count a brand as paying creators only from a real integration

Rule (should): A brand pays creators when the creator talks about it on camera for about a minute, the video carries YouTube's paid-promotion label, and the brand's link sits at the top of the description, often with a campaign or partnership tracking URL. A list of product links with a commission note, a logo banner in the background, or gear in shot without a spoken segment is affiliate or a long-term channel deal, not proof of a sponsorship budget. A code or custom link does not mean commission only: flat-fee sponsorships use them too. Brands that work on commission alone are not worth the pipeline (BRD-QUAL-14).
Do: A product shown without a sponsorship label may simply be a gift the creator liked; don't count it as a paid deal
Source: coaching call "28/7" (2025-07-28) — "Sponsored things are usually at the top of the description, and it gives you a link, and when you click on it, you can see in the URL something about campaign, partnership, things like that."
Source: coaching call "28/7" (2025-07-28) — "easiest way to check if that product is sponsored is just check on his video, go quickly, skim through. Does he talk about this product for like 60 seconds?"
