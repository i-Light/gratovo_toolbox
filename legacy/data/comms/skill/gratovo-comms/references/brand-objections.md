# Brands — Objections

### BRD-OBJ-01 — A wrong fit is not an objection to handle

Rule (must): If the brand said its budget is $10K and you send a $15K creator, no wording fixes it. Objection handling is for doubts about an offer that makes sense; a misfit gets replaced, not argued.
Source: 3.1. How To Make Offers That Close Instantly — "If they tell you their budget is $10K, and you send someone for $15K — you’re not handling objections. You’re ignoring them."
Source: lesson video "3.1. How To Make Offers That Close Instantly" @11:02 — "Objection handling is someone when someone struggles to understand that this makes sense for them."

### BRD-OBJ-02 — Pre-handle objections before the price

Rule (must): Answer likely concerns (fit, spending range, deliverables) before naming a price: before the price it is information, after it every answer sounds like selling.
Source: 3.5. Pre-HandlingHandling objection Aikido Mastery — "by handling a concern before the price drop, it's not really an objection. They can still change their mind."
Source: 3.5. Pre-HandlingHandling objection Aikido Mastery — "Always handle objections early. If you wait until after dropping the price, they’ll assume you’re just trying to sell them, and the conversation will feel disingenuous."

### BRD-OBJ-03 — Most objections hide another concern — dig

Rule (must): Treat an objection as a smokescreen: ask a clarifying question (what is holding you back? is it the price or the upfront payment? what would have made it work?). After a firm no, a polite 'what turned you off, for our own learning?' often reveals the real issue.
Source: 3.6. PART 2 Objection Handling Full Training — "Objections often aren’t real but serve as a smokescreen for underlying concerns."
Source: 3.6. PART 2 Objection Handling Full Training — "Instead of reacting defensively, dig deeper by asking clarifying questions. - Example Questions: "Can you tell me more about what's holding you back?" "Is it the price itself or the upfront payment that"

### BRD-OBJ-04 — Isolate the objection, confirm it is the only one, then solve it with facts

Rule (must): B2B objections are logistical, not emotional: work out whether it is price, timing, fit or trust; check that fixing it would clear the way; then answer with facts, data and examples.
Source: 3.6. PART 2 Objection Handling Full Training — "Unlike B2C, objections in B2B are less emotional and more logistical."
Source: 3.6. PART 2 Objection Handling Full Training — "Isolate the Objection - Figure out the specific objection: is it about price, timing, fit, or trust? - Once isolated, make sure if this thing is fixed, nothing is stopping them from moving forward"

### BRD-OBJ-05 — 'We only work with exclusive agencies'

Rule (should): Mention exclusivity arrangements only if they exist; otherwise answer with a strong offer and a close, relationship-driven way of working (a few creators and brands, the creator wants this collaboration — if true).
Source: 3.6. PART 2 Objection Handling Full Training — "Brands often prefer exclusive influencers to maintain authenticity and avoid overlap with competitors. How to Respond: - Highlight Exclusivity Plans (if applicable): "We understand the importance of exclusivity. [Influencer Name] has signed agreements with us, and after this collaboration, they will be exclusively representing brands like yours in this category." (Only say this if it"
Source: lesson video "3.2. Typical Brand Responses and What to Do" @20:32 — "Oh, just curious, are you opposed to working with non-exclusive influencers, or is it a requirement for the influencer to be exclusive?"

### BRD-OBJ-06 — 'The price is too high'

Rule (must): Find out why first (total price, upfront payment, or ROI doubt). First-time nerves: propose a trial structure. Too high overall: ask what they paid for similar campaigns. Then raise perceived value (authority, engagement, the effective CPM). If they have the money but want a lower unit price: a bundle paid as each video goes live, or a pay-on-performance (CPM) deal.
Source: 3.6. PART 2 Objection Handling Full Training — "The key is to understand why they think it’s too high."
Source: 3.6. PART 2 Objection Handling Full Training — "Would it help if we structured the pricing differently for a trial campaign?"

### BRD-OBJ-07 — 'Let's revisit after Q4'

Rule (should): Create urgency without pushing: if the creator's slots really are limited, offer to lock a slot now with payment later, or simply pencil the creator in for the month they name, with the details settled nearer the time; otherwise ask when to come back and promise not to bother them before — then follow up exactly then.
Source: 3.6. PART 2 Objection Handling Full Training — "The goal here is to create urgency without being pushy."
Source: 3.6. PART 2 Objection Handling Full Training — "When would be a better time to revisit this? I’ll make sure not to bother you before then."

### BRD-OBJ-08 — 'This creator doesn't make sense for us'

Rule (must): Agree, ask what collaborations, metrics or kind of creator they look for, and come back with a better match from the roster.
Source: 3.6. PART 2 Objection Handling Full Training — "Sometimes the influencer isn't a fit, and that's okay. Use this opportunity to learn more about the brand's goals."
Source: 3.6. PART 2 Objection Handling Full Training — "Can you share more about what type of collaborations or metrics you look for?"

### BRD-OBJ-09 — 'We only do affiliate'

Rule (should): Propose a hybrid: a small flat fee to cover production plus an affiliate part. Address the fear behind it (bad upfront experiences) with performance safeguards such as view milestones — only safeguards the agency really offers, and case studies only if real.
Source: 3.6. PART 2 Objection Handling Full Training — "We can structure this as a hybrid deal, with a small flat rate to cover production costs and an affiliate component for performance."
Source: 3.6. PART 2 Objection Handling Full Training — "Affiliate deals are a pain for agencies and we avoid them, but sometimes there are ways to salvage the opportunity."

### BRD-OBJ-10 — 'We don't work with agencies'

Rule (should): Present the agency as small and personal — the people managing this creator's collaborations — and prove it with the research you did on their audience and the fit.
Source: 3.6. PART 2 Objection Handling Full Training — "Some brands avoid agencies, fearing high costs or impersonal service."
Source: 3.6. PART 2 Objection Handling Full Training — "Your goal is to appear as a small, approachable partner. How to Respond: - Position yourself as an extension of the influencer: "I'm not a big agency; I'm simply helping [Influencer Name] manage their collaborations"

### BRD-OBJ-11 — 'The creator already has an agency'

Rule (should): If the creator really asked you to handle this deal, say so and offer proof (a note from them); without that approval, there is no answer to give.
Source: 3.6. PART 2 Objection Handling Full Training — "[Influencer Name] specifically asked us to handle this deal for them, so we have their full support."
Source: 3.6. PART 2 Objection Handling Full Training — "Here’s a note from [Influencer Name] confirming their interest in us handling this collaboration."

### BRD-OBJ-12 — 'We'd rather work directly with creators'

Rule (should): Show the work the agency takes off their plate — finding the right creators, contracts, timelines, deadlines, approvals — and say that the creator works through the agency, asking if that is a problem.
Source: 3.6. PART 2 Objection Handling Full Training — "Some brands think working directly with influencers will save them money."
Source: 3.6. PART 2 Objection Handling Full Training — "We actually handle all the details— finding the right influencers, handling the contracts, timelines, making sure everything is done within the deadlines, content approvals etc"

### BRD-OBJ-13 — 'Join our ambassador program'

Rule (should): Ambassador programs are usually affiliate deals in disguise: welcome it, but ask for a flat fee to cover production (or a hybrid), or offer category exclusivity in exchange for a flat fee.
Source: 3.6. PART 2 Objection Handling Full Training — "Ambassador programs are often just a wrapper for affiliate deals."
Source: lesson video "3.6. PART 2 Objection Handling Full Training" @52:50 — "Ambassador programs are often just a wrapper for affiliate deals."

### BRD-OBJ-14 — A brand's no: ask what was missing, then fix the shortlist

Rule (must): Ask what was missing and act on it: niche mismatch → closer creators; audience too small → bigger creators; too big (usually budget) → nano or micro creators; style mismatch → more informative creators with a mature audience; budget → never cut the rate, offer cheaper creators. After a second pass without clear feedback, move them to a follow-up list — never close the door.
Source: 4.1. Working With the Brand - Coach Ammar — "Budget, don't cut your rate. Offer cheaper creators instead."
Source: 4.1. Working With the Brand - Coach Ammar — "If they pass a second time with no clear feedback, move them to a follow-up list and stop spending time there."

### BRD-OBJ-15 — Confirm everything else before solving the objection

Rule (must): Before fixing an objection, check it is the only blocker: 'apart from budget, does everything else work for you?', then probe product, image, timeline, trust. Use a conditional close: 'if we adjust the payment terms, would you move forward?'
Source: 5.Effective Objection Handling in Sales — "Before addressing any objection, ensure that it’s the only reason blocking the deal."
Source: 5.Effective Objection Handling in Sales — ""Budget aside, does everything else about the collaboration work for you?""

### BRD-OBJ-16 — After a no: the two-choice question

Rule (must): Acknowledge calmly ('no problem, totally understand'), never argue or plead. Then ask a question with two options where one is obviously not the case ('against influencer marketing entirely, or was it just this creator?') so they name the real reason, and adjust.
Source: 5.Turn a 'No' into 'Yes' with This Simple Technique — "Offer them two options where one is obviously wrong. - This makes them reveal their real reason without feeling pressured."
Source: 5.Turn a 'No' into 'Yes' with This Simple Technique — "are you completely against influencer marketing, or was it just this specific creator that wasn’t the right fit?"

### BRD-OBJ-17 — 'Your creator has never been sponsored — why?'

Rule (should): Answer the concern behind the question, not just the question. If there is a true reason that reads well (the creator is selective, turns down what does not fit), give it. Otherwise ask what worries them about it — usually whether the creator can sell — and answer that: an audience that has never been sold to tends to trust the first recommendation more. Every claim must be true of this creator.
Source: lesson video "1.5. Marketing crash course" @8:23 — "Also, what you can do is, why is your guy never sponsored? And instead of answering this question, if you don't have an answer, you can ask a question about the question. Why would the fact that my influencer is never sponsored would be an issue? Can you tell me that?"
Source: lesson video "1.5. Marketing crash course" @9:35 — "The audience trusts him because he never sold anything."

### BRD-OBJ-18 — Turned down for a cheaper creator with more views? It was the audience

Rule (should): When a brand refuses a cheap creator with big views and takes a pricier one with fewer, it is telling you the audience did not fit (age, buying power, why they watch). Do not argue the CPM; look at who watches, and bring a creator whose audience matches.
Source: lesson video "8.Bonus Upselling influencers with different niche" @11:12 — "Even if it's more expensive, even if he gets less views, they would go with him because he makes sense, right?"

### BRD-OBJ-19 — Ask what would need to be in place for them to buy

Rule (should): When a decision maker is interested but unsure (for example a CEO new to YouTube integrations), ask what would need to be in place for this to make sense to them. They will name their real condition: views they can count on, the right audience, the right countries, a return on the spend. Answer exactly that, using only what we can honour (BRD-OFFER-14). A condition that adds work, like picking only creators with a US audience, can be priced as an extra. A request for safety is never the moment to upsell.
Source: lesson video "7.2026.00.00.Abu Lahya 2026 Q&A PT 2" @57:04 — "What needs to be in place for you to want to buy this campaign? Like, what needs to be in place for that to make sense for you? What needs to happen for you to want to move forward? What needs to be in place? And then he'll tell you what's the main objection and what's most needed."
Source: lesson video "7.2026.00.00.Abu Lahya 2026 Q&A PT 2" @57:40 — "Leads from America instead of all over the world. Great. That's an extra thousand dollars because we're going to pick the best influences."

### BRD-OBJ-20 — A format that can't reach their goal: they choose

Rule (should): If a brand insists on a format that won't meet its goal (short-form video for conversions, for instance), say so plainly and let them choose: change the format to reach the goal, or keep the format and measure it against a goal it can reach (reach, awareness). Never promise a result the format can't deliver.
Source: lesson video "7.2026.00.00.Abu Lahya 2026 Q&A PT 1" @40:43 — "That's okay, then ch tell them to change the KPI for something else on conversion. Okay. Optimize for something else. I'm not going to break the rules. I'm not going to change the rules of marketing because you don't know how it works."

### BRD-OBJ-21 — 'How do we know you work with this creator?': show it, don't argue

Rule (should): When a brand doubts that we really represent a creator, or fears an agency scam, answer with proof rather than words: we hold the creator's analytics and agreed rate, a screenshot of the creator's written OK to pitch them (with any prices hidden), or the creator's written confirmation if they ask for it. Add that nothing is paid until the video is live, and offer a short call so they see a real person. Never copy the creator into the thread (BRD-REPLY-24), and never claim exclusive management we don't have (BRD-REPLY-04).
Source: coaching call "September 18: Q&A - Younes" (2025-09-18) — "try to find a screenshot you can take, like an email you can take, where it kind of confirms that you can reach out for him or that you're working with them without revealing the price"
Source: coaching call "September 18: Q&A - Younes" (2025-09-18) — "then all you need to do is reach out to the influencer, and ask for something written"

### BRD-OBJ-22 — 'What deals have you done?': the truth, then the creators' record

Rule (must): If a brand asks about the agency's past deals and there are none yet, say so plainly, then turn to what they really want to know: the creators, hand-picked, with their engagement and the brands they have worked with (as the creator's record, never as ours). Keep the conversation on the creators and the return they can bring. Don't volunteer what nobody asked, but never claim deals, clients, a team or partner agencies we don't have, and never promise results. A brand that will only work with a registered company is a reason to register one, not to bluff.
Don't: Present a creator's past sponsorships as deals the agency brokered; Promise guaranteed results
Source: coaching call "24/7" (2025-07-24) — "give them the bad news, no, I don't have any at the moment, but these influencers, they're handpicked"
Source: coaching call "November 14th 2025 - Advanced Brands (Osama)" (2025-11-14) — "you can refer it more as to him, right? Instead of like, you've done them. So this guy has done X with X, etc."

### BRD-OBJ-23 — 'We'll get back to you' means we get back to them

Rule (should): 'We'll reach out if this changes' and 'not looking for creators right now' are polite noes: brands get dozens of emails a day and rarely come back by themselves. Answer briefly, ask when would be a good time to reconnect (adding, truthfully, that the creator may be booked by then), note the date, and write again in the same thread when it comes. When they name a quarter or a launch month, write a few weeks before it rather than on the day: campaigns are planned ahead, so offer to plan the creator's schedule with them. After a clear no from one employee, don't move straight on to their colleagues.
Source: coaching call "October 4th: Review /Roast my Email - Ammar" (2025-10-04) — "This is just a nice way to say I'm not interested. We will get back. You know, if this will come, never happens."
Source: coaching call "October 9th: Influencers Q&A - Younes" (2025-10-09) — "let it go and uh try to reach out again when the time comes or let them reach out to you uh when the time comes but from my experience usually when they say that very rarely do they actually get back yes you should follow you should"

### BRD-OBJ-24 — 'His views go up and down': explain, place well, or guarantee

Rule (should): When a brand points at a creator's uneven views, find out why the low videos were low (an off-topic video, a dedicated video, a weak thumbnail) and say so honestly; propose the integration in the kind of video that performs (their core topic, not a dedicated video). If the doubt remains, offer a view guarantee at a level the creator has agreed to (BRD-NEG-25) rather than cutting the price.
