# Brands — The offer

### BRD-OFFER-01 — The offer must make sense from the brand's side

Rule (must): Brands say no to offers that do not fit, not to wording: wrong creator, overpriced, low views, bad comments, mismatched content. Look through the brand's eyes (its audience, past creators, content that works for it) and frame the offer as: who the creator is, the content idea, why it fits the brand's goal, and how it drives sales, awareness or downloads.
Source: 3.1. How To Make Offers That Close Instantly — "If your offer is good, you don’t need fancy scripts."
Source: 3.1. How To Make Offers That Close Instantly — "It’s not about saying the right sentence. It’s about the offer making sense."

### BRD-OFFER-02 — Come in under the budget

Rule (should): When a brand names a budget, propose something below it (a $10K budget might get a $6-8K offer): easier to close, room for the brand to breathe, room to upsell later.
Source: 3.1. How To Make Offers That Close Instantly — "If the brand says they want to spend $10K — you don’t have to spend the full $10K."
Source: 3.1. How To Make Offers That Close Instantly — "Stay within or under their budget if possible"

### BRD-OFFER-03 — Offer checklist

Rule (must): Before sending any offer: check the brand's previous campaigns, know its audience and goals, pick a creator that fits style, audience and budget, make sure the content idea helps the brand, stay within budget, explain why it makes sense, and show engagement, view consistency and good comments.
Source: 3.1. How To Make Offers That Close Instantly — "Offer Checklist Before sending your offer: 1. Check the brand's previous campaigns 2. Understand their audience and goals 3. Pick an influencer who fits their style, audience, and budget 4. Make sure the content idea actually helps the brand 5. Stay within or under their budget if possible 6. Clearly explain why your offer makes sense 7. Show engagement, view consistency, and good comments 8. Show you're not just pushing something random"

### BRD-OFFER-04 — Clear deliverables, 2-3 priced options

Rule (must): Always define what the collaboration includes (length, platform, format, why it fits). Offer 2-3 deliverables with clear prices so the brand can choose; one can be clearly the strongest to guide the choice. This is the default of house choice HC-26; the alternative is BRD-OFFER-20.
Source: 3.2. Typical Brand Responses and What to Do — "Lack of Clarity on Deliverables: Always define what’s included in your proposed collaboration"
Source: 3.2. Typical Brand Responses and What to Do — "Suggest 2–3 deliverables with clear pricing to make it easier for brands to choose."

### BRD-OFFER-05 — Match the offer to the brand's objective

Rule (must): Work out what the brand is buying — awareness (impressions, views, engagement), leads (clicks, sign-ups), sales (conversions, ROI, codes), a launch (buzz, mentions) or market testing (feedback) — and frame the creator and the content idea around that goal and its KPIs.
Source: 3.3. Brands and their objectives — "Brands invest in influencer marketing to accomplish specific goals, and your job is to align your pitch and strategy with those objectives."
Source: lesson video "1.4. Sales crash course" @18:09 — "When you're a software company, you want to go to a small influencer that is super niche down on that industry so that whenever they talk about something, they go to him."

### BRD-OFFER-06 — Speak to the reader's job

Rule (must): A marketing manager has KPIs and a budget: make their job easier and show the ROI potential against their goal. A brand manager guards image: show the creator fits the voice. PR wants buzz. A founder wants to feel understood. Small brands: the founder decides; large brands: the marketing manager, within budget.
Source: 3.3. Brands and their objectives — "Position yourself as someone who makes their job easier by delivering results."
Source: 3.3. Brands and their objectives — "If they have $50k left for Q4 and want to sellout 1000 products with this, your job is to show them how a $10k influencer campaign can help achieve at least 20% of that if not more, for it to make a lot of sense for them. - You don't have to dive into the details of the numbers, but make sure to show them the ROI potential is heavy and secured."

### BRD-OFFER-07 — Fit the idea to the campaign type

Rule (should): Launch campaigns want first impressions and curiosity; seasonal promotions want time-bound codes and urgency (Black Friday, holidays); awareness wants reach; conversion wants a trackable code or link. Tailor the content idea to which one the brand is running.
Source: 3.3. Brands and their objectives — "The type of campaign a brand is running will dictate their goals, expectations, and budget."

### BRD-OFFER-08 — Small budget: add value instead of cutting price

Rule (tip): For a tight budget, propose bonus content or a guarantee rather than only discounting — only what the creator has agreed to and can deliver.
Source: 3.3. Brands and their objectives — "If the brand has a smaller budget, propose bonus content or guarantees."

### BRD-OFFER-09 — Find the angle that makes them buy — truthfully

Rule (must): Ask why this brand would buy: has the creator promoted something similar (fresh audience, or proof it worked)? does the audience match? does it engage with related posts? Look for a story that leads naturally to the product (a creator who just moved to a standing desk → an ergonomic keyboard). Never stretch a fit that is not there.
Source: 3.4. How to Negotiate with Brands — "Show the Good Angles (Make it Make Sense for Them) - Before pitching, ask yourself: - Why would they buy this? - How does this collaboration make sense for their goals? - Example: If the brand sells keyboards, think: - Has the influencer promoted a keyboard recently? - (If not, it's fresh and they're ready to buy ✅) - (If yes, you can showcase how good it went"
Source: 3.4. How to Negotiate with Brands — "Obviously don't lie tho, don't start trying to sell ice cream in Alaska and saying this would be game changer, you'll look stupid."

### BRD-OFFER-10 — Start with one test integration

Rule (must): With a new brand, offer a single video or integration — low risk for them, proof for you — and keep that first deal in four figures (coaches start under about $5,000) unless the brand already spends big. Talk about bigger packages (3-5 videos, several creators) only after the first video is out; a package is priced a little below the single-video rate, never with a discount so large it makes the single price look inflated.
Source: 3.5. The foot in the door method — "Begin with one video or one integration. It’s low-risk for the brand and gives you a chance to show what you can deliver."
Source: 3.5. The foot in the door method — "but don't talk about this until the first video is out ! otherwise you'll come off as a greedy middle man just here to milk them for their money !"

### BRD-OFFER-11 — The first campaign must perform

Rule (must): The upsell depends on the first result, so the first creator must fit the brand's audience, content and buying power (location, age); avoid audiences in low-purchasing-power regions unless that is what the brand wants.
Source: 3.5. The foot in the door method — "If you want to make real money, the first campaign has to deliver."
Source: 3.5. The foot in the door method — "Avoid influencers whose audience is mainly in low-purchasing power regions unless it’s exactly what the brand wants."

### BRD-OFFER-12 — Read the brand: CPM test or flat fee

Rule (should): A hesitant or tight brand gets a single-video CPM test (e.g. pay the creator a lower CPM than the brand pays). A confident brand gets a simple flat fee above the creator's cost. Early on aim for about $500-1K profit per deal, but take less on a first deal when it buys trust.
Source: 3.5. The foot in the door method — "CPM Model: Offer a pay-per-view/impression deal. It’s low-risk for them because they pay based on actual results."
Source: 3.5. The foot in the door method — "When you're starting out, aim to make at least $500-$1k profit per deal, but don't be greedy."

### BRD-OFFER-13 — Present the value, not the price

Rule (must): Lead with why this creator is right for this brand (reach, engagement, audience alignment) and how it serves their goal; state deliverables and expected results so saying yes is easy.
Source: 3.SECRET SAUCE Brand Negotiation - Coach Kushtrim — "Show the brand why THIS influencer is perfect for THEM. Explain the reach, engagement, and audience alignment"
Source: 3.SECRET SAUCE Brand Negotiation - Coach Kushtrim — "Communicate what you're offering, explain the deliverables, and show the expected results. Just make it easy for them to say yes"

### BRD-OFFER-14 — No case studies yet: lower their risk — only with what we can honor

Rule (must): Without case studies, trust comes from an offer that leaves the brand little to lose: a single test, a pay-per-view structure, a make-good the creator has already agreed to. Every guarantee, refund or free extra is agreed with the creator and approved by a person before it is offered, and the word 'guarantee' stays out of cold emails (FND-WRITE-12).
Source: lesson video "1.4. Sales crash course" @12:56 — "if you don't have case studies, what you can do to build trust is simply going to the brand and making them a no-brainer offer."
Source: lesson video "1.4. Sales crash course" @14:34 — "And what basically a no-brainer offer is, is an offer that solves their problem. And an offer that doesn't have risk on the end or very minimal risk, so they know they can't lose much, but they can win big."

### BRD-OFFER-15 — Offer the deal model that answers their fear

Rule (should): Ask the brand what worries them about the deal, or whether they would rather play safe or go for the bigger upside, then offer the model that answers it. Safe: a CPM deal, so they pay only for the views delivered (at $20 per thousand views, a million views costs $20K). Upside on a small budget: a flat fee, which is cheap if the video does well. Afraid of missing a target: a view guarantee with an agreed make-good (BRD-OFFER-14). Agree first with the creator which models they accept, let the brand choose, then confirm the chosen one with the creator.
Source: lesson video "7.2026.00.00.Abu Lahya 2026 Q&A PT 2" @1:35:55 — "Would you rather take a safe route or the more risky route? If they still want to take the safe route, say great, then we can look in a CPM price when you only get paid for the views you get"
Source: lesson video "7.2026.00.00.Abu Lahya 2026 Q&A PT 2" @1:37:31 — "what's the biggest fear you guys have with going with this deal? Like, what's the biggest issue? They're gonna say, I'm afraid that I'm not gonna get enough views and I'm gonna lose money. that's when you bring in the view guarantee. Basically you want to use your different offers as solutions to their problems."

### BRD-OFFER-16 — A stronger offer, not a cheaper one: four levers

Rule (should): An offer gets easier to accept when it promises a bigger outcome (views, clicks, sales), makes that outcome more likely (a creator who fits, a track record, a make-good), delivers it sooner, and asks less of the brand (we handle everything). Strengthen one of these levers before cutting the price, and promise only what can be delivered.
Source: lesson video "7.2026.00.00.Abu Lahya 2026 Q&A PT 1" @27:35 — "it's the dream outcome times the perceived likelihood of achievement decreased by the time delay and the effort and sacrifice."
Source: lesson video "7.2026.00.00.Abu Lahya 2026 Q&A PT 1" @28:06 — "Best offer is like going to get the millions of views. They're not going to have to spend a lot of money for it. You're going to handle everything. They won't have to click a single button. And you're starting the campaign today."

### BRD-OFFER-17 — Never criticise their marketing or claim what their audience needs

Rule (must): A brand already paying creators has a marketing team; telling it what it lacks, or that a past campaign reached the wrong people, can insult the people reading. Speak to what they want next (awareness, a launch, a new audience) and why our creator helps, framed as an opening, never as a gap in their work. Never state as fact what their customers or the creator's viewers do, use or need, or how one of their campaigns performed, unless the case says so.
Don't: Say a past campaign of theirs reached the wrong audience or underperformed; Write that the creator's viewers need or use their product when the case does not say so

Source: coaching call "Sep 5th- RME with Eyoub" (2026-09-05) — "For brands, it depends how you, you know, you're free. Is it? It might came out as an insult. Like, hey, you don't have this, and they have the whole sales team. Or this, they have the whole marketing team."

### BRD-OFFER-18 — A cold offer gives one strong reason, not a stat sheet

Rule (should): In a first email to a brand, give the one reason this creator would make them money, with the single figure that proves it: the views on the kind of video they would sponsor, the audience country that matters to them, an engagement rate stated next to the views, or sponsors that keep coming back. Leave out subscriber counts (subscribers don't watch the ad) and the full list of followers, engagement, age and gender that every agency sends. Round figures the way people say them ('60k-plus views'). The full numbers go out when the brand asks (BRD-PRES-09).
Source: coaching call "3/1 - Roast my emails (Brands) w/Ammar" (2026-01-03) — "In my opinion, guys, generally, talking about subscribers make no sense. You know, he has 7 million subscribers. So what, bro? He's making 40K views."
Source: coaching call "3/4 - Brands Q&A" (2026-04-03) — "don't focus, don't propose everything in your email at the same time, so don't say, hey, I've got this influencer because he has this audience, he has this demos, he has this sort of age, right, so just focus on one thing"

### BRD-OFFER-19 — Price to what the brand sells and to the creator's size

Rule (should): The CPM a brand will pay depends on what it sells and on how big the creator is. Software and core tech can carry the top of the tech range (around $50 CPM); accessories and low-priced physical products sit nearer $25-30, because brands with high production costs keep less for marketing. Creators averaging 200k+ views get a lower CPM, and a total that runs to tens of thousands on a first deal gets ghosted rather than negotiated. Every figure is proposed for a person to approve (INF-PRICE-06).
Source: coaching call "10/4 - Brands Q&A" (2026-04-10) — "You can't pitch a 50 CPM to a brand that does accessories, for example, that does, let's say, phone cases, or does screen protectors. You can't pitch that to them, right? If you're gonna go, like, let's say, for example, software related, yeah, sure, you can do 50 CPM."
Source: coaching call "1/7" (2025-07-01) — "Brands that will give you good money are brands that will spend not too much on the production of whether it is their software, their product, and stuff like that."

### BRD-OFFER-20 — Coach Ahmed: one clear offer, not a menu of prices

Rule (tip): Coach Ahmed gives a brand one option, the standard integration, even when it asks about a dedicated video as well: a list of prices for every format gives them too many choices and invites the cheapest. One of the options of house choice HC-26; the default is the lesson's two or three priced options (BRD-OFFER-04).

### BRD-OFFER-21 — A shorter integration is not half the price

Rule (should): When a brand wants a 30-second spot instead of 60-90 seconds, don't halve the price: the creator's production and audience stay the same. Price it at roughly two-thirds to three-quarters of the full integration (about $4,000-4,500 against $6,000), for a person to approve.
Source: coaching call "27/6" (2025-06-27) — "I wouldn't split, I wouldn't split it literally. I would still, I'm a bit cheeky so I would still add it. to it right so for example if the 60 to 90 second integration is uh six thousand the 30 to 60 i'll make it four thousand or four thousand five hundred"

### BRD-OFFER-22 — No 'last slot' urgency in a pitch

Rule (must): Don't use the creator's open slot or 'one spot left this month' as urgency in a pitch: brands plan on their own campaign calendar, a deal takes weeks (contract, brief, draft approval), and a mid-size creator is not scarce, so it reads as needy or made up. When availability matters, state it plainly over the coming months (a slot in February, one in March). Real timing that fits their calendar, such as a season or Black Friday, is fine. Never claim the creator is excited about their product (BRD-FIRST-09).

Like if he has free slot right now and you are doing like, I don't know, the brand is doing swimwear or something like that. Make no sense. Okay, he has free slot in March. So what? Like, no, it's not swimming season."

### BRD-OFFER-23 — Pitch a creator as one of several, and name the sub-niche we cover

Rule (should): In a pitch, present the named creator (one, two at most) as one of the creators we work with, and say which sub-niche we cover. A brand that doesn't like that creator, or was already pitched them by another agency, then asks to see others instead of going silent. Only say what is true of our roster (BRD-FIRST-16): 'a couple of creators' only when we have them.
Source: coaching call "February 14th: Roast my email (Brands)- Ammar" (2026-02-14) — "Usually, Like, we work with a couple of influencers, I like to say including this one, and I mention it. So if they are not interested in this guy, they would not say, oh, no, no, no, we don't like this guy, it just goes as email, because that would usually be what happens."
Source: coaching call "August 22nd- Roast my email- Ammar" (2026-08-22) — "Just in case maybe this is not the right fit, maybe someone else pitched this influencer before you. You have to keep that in mind as well. You're not the only one who's working with him."
